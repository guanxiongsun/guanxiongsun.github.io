A diffusion model for 3D sign language production from text

Official project page

In one paragraph

Sign language production needs expressive hands, bodies and faces, not 2D keypoints or glosses. Neural Sign Actors builds what the authors describe as the first large-scale 3D dataset of American Sign Language, by reconstructing How2Sign with SMPL-X through a fitting pipeline constrained by pose priors. It then trains a text-conditioned diffusion model whose denoiser is an anatomically informed graph network on the SMPL-X skeleton. It generates 3D signing avatars directly from text, and fluent ASL signers judged the results in a user study.

The problem

Sign languages are the main way of communicating for more than 70 million Deaf and Hard of Hearing people. Deep learning has moved sign language recognition and translation forward quickly. Production, turning text into signing, is harder: the output has to look natural and carry exact meaning, through the shape, orientation, position and motion of the hands, and through the arms and the face.

Most production methods generate 2D skeletons, and their output is limited in how expressive the hands and body can be. The obvious fix, generating a full 3D body with articulated fingers and a face, had no data to learn from: there was no large-scale dataset pairing text with 3D signing avatars.1

1 The avatars on this page use SMPL-X (Pavlakos et al., CVPR 2019), a parametric 3D body model with articulated hands and an expressive face.

Figures and challenges as stated on the CVPR 2024 poster.

Data: a 3D version of How2Sign

So the first step was data. How2Sign (Duarte et al., CVPR 2021) is a large corpus of continuous American Sign Language video with English transcripts. The team fitted the SMPL-X model, body, hands and face together, to its frames, turning the corpus into a dataset of 4D signing avatars.

With 3D data in hand, the model learns to generate signing straight from a sentence, with no glosses in between. How it works

Neural Sign Actors is a diffusion model over whole signing sequences. A sequence p1:F holds, for every frame, the SMPL-X joint rotations θ and the facial expression parameters ψ. Training adds noise to it step by step; the network learns to predict that noise, so at test time it can start from pure noise and denoise its way to a new sequence, conditioned on the sentence.

Inside the denoiser (Fig. 4), the noisy joint rotations get a learned per-joint pose embedding and go through a graph neural network defined on the SMPL-X skeleton, so messages pass along the skeleton and a finger joint is encoded together with the wrist and arm it hangs from. The facial expressions take a parallel path through their own embedding and an MLP. The transcript is encoded by CLIP, and the CLIP encoding conditions the model.

The per-frame features x1…xF then pass through an auto-regressive RNN, which carries information across frames, and an MLP regression head turns them back into pose θ1:F and expression ψ1:F. Training minimises the usual noise-prediction loss, 𝓛t = ‖εt − εθ(pt1:F, t, w1:F)‖2. At inference the model starts from zT ∼ 𝒩(0, I) and runs the reverse process to produce a new signing sequence for any sentence.

Key idea

The denoiser works on the skeleton graph, so finger predictions are conditioned through the bones on the wrist and arm.

Results

On How2Sign, Neural Sign Actors is compared with four earlier sign-language production baselines on four errors: vertex and joint position (MPVPE, MPJPE), a distribution distance (FID) and a sequence-alignment distance (DTW).2 It has the lowest error on all four.

Signers in the loop

Numbers on poses do not say whether the signing is understandable. In a user study, 15 ASL-fluent participants rated how well each clip aligns with its transcript: the original video, its 3D fit, and the generations of Stoll et al. and Neural Sign Actors.

Signing avatars are a demanding case of a general problem: generating human motion from language, with the hands and timing right. Robots that learn from people face the same problem from the other side, which is where I want to take it next.

Open question

Can the generative machinery behind signing avatars produce robot actions, from the same kind of scarce, expensive demonstrations?
